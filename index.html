Mnemonic: FIRE (Fine-tuning) — it's about teaching behavior, not facts. Q2: AI Guardrails & Shipping Apr 23 Evening "What are AI guardrails, and how do they change the way you ship a product compared to traditional software?" Strong answer hits: Define it crisply: Guardrails are constraints applied to AI inputs, outputs, or behavior to prevent harmful, off-topic, or policy-violating responses. They operate at multiple layers — prompt-level instructions, output classifiers, retrieval filters, and human review queues. SHIELD mnemonic: Safety filters, Human escalation, Input validation, Evaluation loops, Logging/audit trails, Domain boundaries. How it changes shipping: Traditional software = deterministic QA (test cases pass/fail). AI products = probabilistic — you can't enumerate all outputs. So you need: (1) red-teaming before launch, (2) production monitoring with automated flagging, (3) a "blast radius" mindset — launch to smaller audiences first, expand as guardrail confidence grows. Product tradeoffs: Tighter guardrails = fewer harmful outputs but also more false refusals (users get frustrated when the AI won't answer reasonable questions). Finding the right threshold is a product decision, not just an engineering one. Real examples: Meta's Llama Guard (input/output classifier), OpenAI's system prompt constraints, Anthropic's Constitutional AI approach. Bonus: Guardrails need to be language- and culture-aware — what's fine in one market may be offensive in another. Especially relevant for global products. Inference Cost Curves & Jevons Paradox (AI Economics) Apr 24 Morning Concept: As model inference gets cheaper (better hardware, distillation, quantization), usage doesn't just grow linearly — it explodes. The PM implication is that your cost model needs to forecast demand elasticity, not just unit cost. A feature that's uneconomical at 10¢/query might become default-on at 0.5¢/query, but the volume spike could 20x your bill anyway. Why it matters: Determines whether you gate an AI feature behind a premium tier, ship it free to drive engagement, or wait a quarter for inference costs to drop — one of the highest-leverage product decisions in AI right now. Soundbite: "I don't just model cost-per-query — I model how demand changes as cost drops, because cheaper inference unlocks use cases that weren't viable before, and the aggregate spend can actually increase." Scenario: You're deciding whether to launch an AI-powered "smart reply" feature across all Meta wearables. At current inference costs, it's $0.8M/month at projected volume. Strong answer hits: What it is: After pre-training (predict next token) and supervised fine-tuning (learn from human-written examples), RLHF adds a third training phase: human raters compare pairs of model outputs and pick the better one. These preferences train a reward model, which then guides the LLM via reinforcement learning (PPO or similar) to generate outputs humans prefer. Why it matters for PMs: RLHF is what makes the difference between "technically correct but weird" and "actually helpful and pleasant to use." It's the bridge from capability to product quality. The preference data IS your product definition — what raters are told to prefer (helpful > harmless > honest, or some other hierarchy) directly encodes product values. PM decision: who are the raters, what are their guidelines, and how do you handle disagreements? Different rater pools = different model personalities. Trade-offs to name: Reward hacking — the model learns to game the reward signal (e.g., generating verbose, confident-sounding answers that score well but aren't actually more accurate). PMs need to monitor for this. Alignment tax — RLHF can make models safer but also more cautious / less creative. Overly-aligned models refuse reasonable requests. Finding the right calibration is a product call. Preference diversity — whose preferences? Western raters may not represent global users. A single reward model can flatten cultural nuance. Newer alternatives: DPO (Direct Preference Optimization) skips the reward model entirely, training on preference pairs directly — simpler, cheaper, increasingly popular. Constitutional AI (Anthropic) uses AI feedback instead of human feedback for some alignment steps. May 8 Evening Position to defend or challenge: "AI agents should be able to take irreversible actions — sending emails, making purchases, booking reservations — without requiring human confirmation every time. The confirmation friction destroys the agent's value proposition." Framework: ARC (Acknowledge → Reframe → Counter) Strong answer hits: Acknowledge: The friction argument is real. If an AI agent has to ask "are you sure?" before every action, it's just a slightly smarter autocomplete. Users will abandon it. The whole promise of agents is delegation — "do this for me" not "draft this for me to review." Reframe: This isn't a binary (always confirm vs. never confirm). It's a trust calibration problem — and the PM's job is designing the right escalation framework. Counter with a tiered model: Low-stakes, reversible: Just do it. Rescheduling a meeting, adding an item to a cart, sending a routine acknowledgment. Undo > confirm. Medium-stakes, semi-reversible: Do it but notify immediately with easy undo window. Sending an email to a known contact, booking a cancelable reservation. "I booked Niku for 7pm Saturday. Undo?" High-stakes, irreversible: Always confirm. Purchases above a threshold, emails to new contacts or large groups, anything involving money transfer, legal commitments, or sensitive data. No shortcut. Confidence-gated: The agent's own confidence score can determine the tier dynamically. High confidence on a routine action → execute. Low confidence → ask. The real product insight: Trust is earned progressively. A new user should see more confirmations. A user who's had an agent for 6 months with zero errors should see fewer. This is the same mental model as credit limits — you start low and increase with demonstrated reliability. Risk framing: One bad irreversible action (wrong person emailed, wrong item purchased, embarrassing message sent) can permanently destroy trust in the product. The cost of one false negative confirmation (user annoyed by a prompt) is vastly lower than the cost of one false positive action (agent does something wrong irreversibly). Asymmetric risk demands asymmetric caution. Competitive angle: This is an active design battleground right now — every major agent platform (OpenAI, Google, Apple) is wrestling with exactly this tradeoff. The company that gets the trust calibration right wins the agent market.
